# Red Pozole

## Ingredients

- 2 pounds (about 5 cups) fresh or frozen nixtamal corn, well rinsed 	OR 1 ½ pounds (4 cups) American Southwestern dried pozole corn
- 1 head garlic, cloves broken apart, peeled and halved
- 3 1/2 pounds (1 1/2 medium) pork shanks, cut into 1 ½-inch thick pieces (you’ll have to ask the butcher to cut this for you)
- 1 1/2 pounds (2 medium) pork trotters (aka fresh pigs’ feet), cut lengthwise in half (you’ll have to ask the butcher to do this for you, too)
- 1 1/2 pounds bone-in pork shoulder, cut into 3 or 4 large pieces  (again, ask the butcher to help)
- 2 large white onions, chopped rather finely
- 8 medium dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
- 3 limes, cut into wedges
- 6 cups thinly sliced cabbage or head lettuce (though not traditional, I love Napa cabbage for pozole)
- 15 radishes, thinly sliced
- 3 to 4 tablsepoons dried Mexican oregano
- 2 tablespoons coarsely ground hot dried red chile (optional), store bought or homemade
- 24 tostadas (crisp-fried corn tortillas) store bought or homemade

## Instructions

1. Getting the corn ready. Seasoned cooks often like to remove the pointed, hard germ from each nixtamal kernel (lime-processed corn) to help the kernels open up into a flower shape as they cook. Using a fingernail, knife, or investing considerable effort will do the job. (This approach won't work with dried American Southwestern pozole.)

2. In a large pot holding about 10 quarts, pour in 6 quarts of water. Put in your corn—whether it's rinsed nixtamal or dried corn—and add garlic. Heat until boiling vigorously, then place a partial lid on top and turn the heat down to medium-low for a slow, steady simmer until the corn becomes completely soft. Budget at least 2 to 3 hours if using nixtamal; dried corn needs around 5 hours. Top up the water as needed to keep the level steady. The extended and slower your cooking goes, the better your pozole will be—this echoes the Mexican custom of cooking massive batches over wood fires all night before festivals.

3. Dealing with the meat. While the corn cooks, work on the meat in another large pot. Place both meats in the pot and cover with 4 quarts water mixed with 2 tablespoons salt. Turn up the heat until it boils, then skim away the gray foam that rises for several minutes, and toss in one of your diced onions. Put a partial lid on and keep the heat at medium-low, simmering until the meat is completely fork-tender—about 2 hours. (If you have time, let the meat cool down in its cooking liquid for superior taste and texture.)

4. Finishing up the meat. After cooling, pull the meat out of the broth. Skim away any extra fat from the broth until you have roughly 2 generous quarts remaining. Pull the pork shank meat into big shreds. Do the same with the shoulder meat. Take the trotters, throw out the bones and knuckles, then cube the cartilage and meat into ½-inch pieces. Toss these cubes with the shredded meat (you should have around 6 cups total). Cover and refrigerate if you won't serve within an hour.

5. Making the sauce base. As the meat and corn simmer away, soak the ancho chiles in hot water to cover—put a small plate on them to keep them underwater—for roughly 20 minutes. Puree the chiles and their soaking liquid in batches in a blender or food processor until completely smooth. When the corn is tender, pass the chile mixture through a fine-mesh sieve right into the corn pot, letting the liquid drip through while catching the papery skin pieces. Mix in the strained pork broth and 1 tablespoon salt. Cover partially and let it simmer another 1 hour.

6. Setting up the toppings. Get ready small separate bowls of condiments for guests to personalize their bowls: lime wedges, shredded lettuce or cabbage, thin-sliced radishes, fresh oregano, and hot chile if wanted. Dump your remaining diced onion into a fine-mesh strainer, rinse it well under cool water, let the water drain off, and put it in a serving dish with the other toppings.

7. Putting it all together and checking seasoning. Add the cooked meat to the simmering pozole and fold it in. Check how it looks—you want it hearty with lots of hominy and meat pieces visible throughout, but still soupy enough to be called either a soup or a meaty broth. Taste it and fix the salt to your liking. Keep in mind that hominy drinks up a lot of salt while cooking, so you might need an extra tablespoon or even more.

8. Serving at the table. Serve the pozole buffet-style, or ladle it into large bowls for each person before bringing out the toppings. Tell your guests to crush their oregano leaves by hand right before they sprinkle them in. Save the crushed chile for people who like more heat. Serve alongside with tostadas.

9. Getting ahead: You can prepare pozole through Step 3 and keep it in the fridge for several days—it often tastes even better. The tricky part is cooling it fast enough—split finished pozole right away into at least four containers with 2 to 3 quarts in each to speed cooling up—plus having enough fridge space. Save Step 4 for right before you're ready to eat.
